THE RESULT: the ground under the rivers is smooth to within a millimetre --
ZERO percent of neighbouring cells differ by even one voxel. The water surface
laid on that ground jumps more than THREE voxels between neighbours a sixth of
the time. The owner's "manmade magenta staircase, not a natural water flow" is
the WATER surface, not the terrain. Every fix so far has adjusted depth, and
|d SURFACE| tracks |d depth| almost exactly (22 vs 20, 496 vs 500) because the
ground contributes nothing: surface = ground + depth, and ground is flat here,
so the surface simply inherits every discontinuity the depth law produces.

WHY DEPTH IS DISCONTINUOUS: depth is assigned per cell from a local law
(Q, slope) and neighbouring cells get materially different values. The
construction is backwards. A river surface is smooth and monotone by physics
and the BED is what varies; deriving surface = ground + f(local Q) inverts that
and hands the surface the bed's noise plus the law's.

RETRACTED IN THE SAME SESSION -- two metrics I reported that do not hold up:

1. "62% of downhill wet steps rise" (uphill_check.py, all three bake versions).
   Real number, misleading quantity. It recomputes steepest descent on the
   shipped ground, and on these reaches the ground drop is p50 4 mm -- ONE
   quantisation step. The descent direction is therefore chosen by rounding
   noise among near-ties, so "downhill" is close to a random direction and a
   surface that varies +-270 mm rises about half the time by construction.
   61.66% is consistent with noise. Do not read it as "62% of the river flows
   uphill". The >1 m tail (9,860 cells) is still meaningful; the headline is not.

2. The downstream/upstream split of those rises (80.6% rising downstream vs
   24.5% upstream, which looked like a physics inversion). It assumed the low 5
   bits of `flow` are log2 discharge. They are not clean discharge:
   corr(q, ground) = +0.108 over channel cells (a discharge field should be
   NEGATIVE -- big rivers sit low), and depth is non-monotone in q
   (q=6 -> 1260mm, q=18 -> 720mm, q=24 -> 2330mm). Discarded. Identify the
   field before splitting anything by it again.

What does survive from that dead end, because it does not depend on direction:
on the steps examined, ground drop was p50 4 mm while depth changed p50 270 mm,
and depth change exceeded ground drop in 100.0% of cases. The height comes from
the depth field, not from the terrain.

NEXT: build the surface first -- smooth and monotone along the network -- then
take depth = surface - ground, instead of the reverse. Measure with this same
script; the target is |d SURFACE| >100mm falling from 32% toward the ground's
0.0%, WITHOUT flattening the long profile (bv17 aggregate gradient 205.7 m/km).

CORRECTION, SAME DAY, BEFORE ANYONE BUILDS ON THE ABOVE.

THE |d ground| COLUMN IS NOT THE GROUND. Retract the headline claim ("the
ground under the rivers is smooth to within a millimetre, so the staircase is
in the water, not the terrain"). It is not established.

The disproof comes from the client, which is the authority on what it draws.
A capture at column (-60688, -51716) logs:

    pose: ... ground top z=1809.2 m

That column lies inside fine tile (-4,-4). Reconstructing that tile in Python
the way this measurement did -- base_offset_mm + elevation_cp * quant -- gives a
tile that spans 2287.5..2312.5 m. The client's ground is ~480 m BELOW the entire
range my reconstruction admits. So `elevation_cp + base_offset` is not the
drawn ground, and a gradient taken from it alone is not the drawn ground's
gradient.

The per-tile reliefs say the same thing without the client's help. Across the
six wet-block tiles the WITHIN-tile relief is 14.19, 15.56, 17.33, 22.03, 25.03
and 41.88 m, while the BETWEEN-tile base offsets run 991 m to 2921 m. Tiles
that share an edge would then differ by hundreds of metres across that edge
while never varying more than ~25 m inside it. No continuous terrain does that.
The relief lives in a component I was not reading.

WHAT STILL STANDS, because it never used ground:
  |d depth| between adjacent wet channel cells is p50 20 mm, p90 500 mm, and
  exceeds one 100 mm voxel for 32.0% of adjacent pairs (bv17). Depth is read
  straight off water_cp at a known LSB. The depth field really does jump
  several voxels between neighbours, and it really did improve bv15 -> bv17
  (35.2% -> 32.0% over one voxel, p90 600 -> 500 mm).

WHAT DOES NOT STAND: that those depth jumps become surface jumps. surface =
ground + depth, and if the true ground falls where depth grows the surface can
be smooth while depth is rough -- which is exactly what a river does, since a
deepening pool sits in a dropping bed. The |d SURFACE| row above is
|d depth| plus a ground term that was ~0 by construction, so it measured
nothing independent.

THE LESSON, and it is the same one as [[voxelsim-draw-path-2k]] and the
retracted transect: THERE ARE THREE GROUNDS -- the sample field, the
reconstructed spline (the water datum), and the amplified surface the player
sees -- and I reimplemented the reconstruction in Python instead of calling the
one the client uses. Do not compute ground in Python for this purpose again.
`vxc_riverribbonprobe` already reconstructs it the client's way and already has
a LONG PROFILE section; measure surface gradient and surface roughness THERE.
